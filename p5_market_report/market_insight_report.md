# UK Gilt Markets and the Limits of Fiscal Credibility
## A Market Insight Report

---

**The core argument:** The September 2022 UK gilt crisis was not primarily a market overreaction to an unfunded fiscal package. It was the market correctly pricing the discovery that the UK had less fiscal space than previously assumed — and that the institutional framework for maintaining that space was weaker than it appeared. The same structural vulnerabilities persist today, and they matter for how the gilt market will behave if the government faces another period of fiscal pressure.

---

### What happened and why it matters

In September 2022, the Truss government's 'mini-budget' announced roughly £45 billion of unfunded tax cuts. Within days, the 30-year gilt yield rose by approximately 150 basis points — an extraordinary move for a developed-market sovereign. The pound fell sharply. Most importantly, the Bank of England was forced to intervene with emergency gilt purchases to prevent a collapse in liability-driven investment (LDI) strategies at UK pension funds.

The standard narrative is that irresponsible fiscal policy caused a market panic. This is partially true but misses the more interesting mechanism. Gilt yields had already been rising throughout 2022 as global monetary policy tightened. What the mini-budget did was act as a *coordination device*: it gave the market permission to reprice UK sovereign risk in a direction it had already been leaning. The speed of the move was exacerbated by the LDI feedback loop — pension funds using leverage against gilts were forced to sell as yields rose, pushing yields higher still, triggering more selling. This is a classic liquidity spiral.

The BoE intervention broke the spiral by providing a buyer of last resort. But the intervention itself was revealing: the central bank had to step in to stabilise the bond market of a developed sovereign. That is not supposed to happen.

---

### The fiscal arithmetic

The UK's fiscal position is worth understanding in its own right, separate from the political noise around the mini-budget.

UK public debt stood at roughly 100% of GDP by 2023 — not unusually high by OECD standards (Japan exceeds 200%, the US is above 120%), but meaningfully higher than the UK's historical norm, and higher than the levels at which markets have historically become sensitive to fiscal slippage.

More importantly, the UK runs a structural primary deficit — the government spends more than it collects in taxes even before debt interest payments. In FY2023/24, debt interest payments alone consumed over £100 billion, approximately 10% of total government spending. This creates a compounding problem: higher yields directly increase the cost of refinancing existing debt, which worsens the deficit, which could put further upward pressure on yields. The feedback loop from the other direction.

The Office for Budget Responsibility's November 2024 forecast illustrated the bind: Reeves's October 2024 budget increased borrowing by around £70 billion per year, leading to gilt yields rising immediately and the OBR revising down its assessment of fiscal headroom. The government had spent its headroom before any external shock had occurred. This is not catastrophic in isolation, but it means the UK enters any future period of financial stress with less buffer than it had in 2019 or 2010.

---

### Why the structural vulnerabilities persist

Three features of the UK fiscal position are genuinely unusual and not always appreciated by commentators focused on the headline debt-to-GDP ratio.

**First, the maturity profile.** The UK has traditionally financed itself with longer-dated gilts than most peers. This was once considered a strength — it reduces rollover risk. But in a rising-rate environment, it means that the interest rate sensitivity of the debt stock is higher: the government locked in low rates for longer, so the full impact of higher rates takes longer to feed through to debt servicing costs, but when it does, it's sustained. The average maturity of UK government debt is around 15 years, significantly longer than the US or Germany.

**Second, index-linked gilts.** Approximately a quarter of UK government debt is linked to RPI inflation — unusual by international standards. During the 2022 inflation surge, the interest cost on this debt rose sharply in real terms. This is a direct transmission mechanism from inflation to fiscal costs that doesn't exist for most sovereigns. It is simultaneously a hedge for gilts investors (attractive during inflation) and a fiscal risk for the Treasury.

**Third, the LDI vulnerability still exists.** The pension fund sector has reduced its leverage following the 2022 crisis, but UK defined-benefit pension funds still hold large quantities of gilts and derivatives. If long-dated yields spike again, the dynamics that forced the BoE's hand in 2022 could reassert themselves in weaker form. The regulatory response has improved resilience but not eliminated the structural concentration of interest rate risk in this sector.

---

### What this means for the gilt market

The gilt market is pricing a risk premium over German Bunds that has been persistently wider since 2022 — a structural repricing of UK sovereign risk that partly reflects the events of that year and partly reflects the genuine uncertainty about long-run UK fiscal trajectory.

Whether that premium is fair or excessive depends on your view of three things: the credibility of UK fiscal institutions (the OBR, the Bank of England's independence, parliamentary fiscal rules), the trajectory of UK growth (which determines how the debt ratio evolves without fiscal effort), and the behaviour of the gilt investor base (whether foreign investors continue to absorb UK issuance at current yields).

The UK cannot grow its way out of its fiscal position at current trend growth rates (roughly 1.5–2% annually). It cannot inflate away the debt easily because approximately a quarter is index-linked. It can either cut spending, raise taxes, or rely on continued investor appetite for gilts — or some combination. The October 2024 budget chose option two (tax rises) but with higher spending growth, leaving the net fiscal stance broadly accommodative.

The market's reaction to the October 2024 budget — gilt yields rising modestly but not catastrophically — suggests investors accepted the fiscal story but demanded slightly higher compensation. This is the equilibrium the UK currently occupies: not in crisis, but not enjoying the fiscal credibility discount that Germany or Switzerland benefit from.

---

### Conclusion

The UK gilt market is not in crisis and a 2022-style event is not imminent. But the structural conditions that made 2022 possible — a narrow fiscal margin, a concentrated LDI sector, high inflation sensitivity via index-linked debt, and low growth — have not been resolved. The gilt market is pricing this correctly, with a risk premium relative to peers that reflects genuine uncertainty rather than irrational panic.

For a policymaker, the implication is that fiscal credibility must be demonstrated through actions not statements — sustained primary surpluses, not fiscal rules that get quietly revised. For an investor, it means UK gilts offer a higher yield than equivalents partly for good structural reasons, and that yield could spike again if any of the three pillars (growth, fiscal discipline, institutional credibility) shows signs of cracking.

Markets in developed sovereign debt are not usually pricing events in isolation; they are pricing the entire *distribution* of future fiscal paths. The UK's distribution is wider and skewed more negatively than it was in 2019. That's what the gilt spread reflects.
